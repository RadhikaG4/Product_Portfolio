# Lead with decisions, prove them with numbers

The PM portfolios that impress hiring managers do three things in the first minute: they say who the candidate is and what kind of PM work they do, they show outcomes with numbers rather than lists of activities, and they make the candidate's own decisions visible, including the options rejected and why. Everything else (page count, nav order, About page, resume link) exists to support a fast skim followed by a deeper read before interviews. The evidence says reviewers spend seconds to about a minute on a first pass and 15 to 60 minutes only once a candidate is shortlisted, so each case study needs a result-first snapshot on top and a decision trail underneath. Portfolios are optional for PMs and uncommon (about 16% of 325 PMs in one 2024 survey had one), but they help most for early-career, APM and career-switching candidates, and adoption is highest in Asia, including India, where some postings ask for one. A caveat on method applies to everything below: **the network proxy blocked full-page fetches for almost every source, so this report rests on search-result summaries of the linked pages, not full reads, and no real PM portfolio site could be opened and examined.** Most of the evidence is practitioner opinion from coaches, hiring managers and course sellers. The only hard data is a resume eye-tracking study and one small self-reported survey. For Radhika Goel, the practical upshot is that she already has the rare ingredients (a real pilot with a funnel, a field insight that changed the product, a constraint-driven design decision, and a side project with real users) and needs to package them result-first, label confidentiality and project type honestly, and fill the numeric gaps in the Country Delight and Elective Planner stories.

## Reviewers skim for seconds, then read deeply only after shortlisting

The timing evidence is weak but consistent in shape. The one controlled study is about resumes, not portfolios: Ladders' 2018 eye-tracking study of 30 recruiters found an **average initial resume screen of 7.4 seconds**, and layouts with clear headings, bold titles and bulleted accomplishments did better than cluttered, multi-column ones with long sentences ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/)). Even that figure is disputed as oversimplified ([Spectacle Talent Partners](https://spectacletalentpartners.com/is-the-6-second-resume-scan-a-myth/)). Portfolio-specific estimates come mostly from design hiring: a **6 to 8 second first scan** where the first 3 seconds go to the opening case study's title and first visual ([UX University](https://newsletter.uxuniversity.io/p/a-hiring-manager-will-spend-6-seconds)), about 30 seconds to reject ([ADPList](https://adplist.substack.com/p/only-30-seconds-to-reject-your-portfolio)), and 2 to 3 minutes for a hiring manager's initial review with about 15 minutes set aside before an interview ([Open Doors Careers](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-recruiters-and-hiring-managers-actually-look-at-your-portfolio)). These numbers conflict because they measure different stages, and none cites a study. A PM-specific version of the same advice says the site must answer who you are, what you have done and how you think "in under 60 seconds" ([Sid Saladi](https://sidsaladi.substack.com/p/114-ship-your-pm-portfolio-website)).

The other mode is the deep read. Aakash Gupta reports a Series C VP of Product spending nearly an hour on one candidate's portfolio before the interview ([Aakash Gupta, Medium](https://aakashgupta.medium.com/only-17-of-pms-have-a-portfolio-heres-how-to-build-one-that-actually-gets-you-hired-3fed3e2f438a)). Attention also decays across projects: one estimate has recruiters reading the first case study for 5 to 10 minutes, the second for 3 to 5 and the third for 1 to 2 ([Aashrey Sharma](https://aashreysharma.com/how-long-should-your-ux-case-study-be)). The design consequence is a layered page. The **title carries the outcome** ("Cut onboarding drop-off 18%", not "Onboarding redesign"), a **snapshot box** gives role, timeline, problem, key decisions and headline metrics in about 30 seconds, and the body carries the reasoning for the interviewer who wants questions to ask. A useful self-test is to have someone scan a case study for 30 seconds, close the tab and say what they remember; if they can name the problem, approach and outcome, it works ([UX Tools](https://www.uxtools.co/blog/5-principles-of-exceptional-case-studies-in-ux-portfolios)). On length, **800 to 1,500 well-structured words plus visuals** is described as enough, and a repetitive 4,000-word case study as weaker than a tight 800-word one ([Design Decade](https://designdecade.org/how-to-write-a-ux-case-study-complete-structure-tips-and-examples-for-2026/)). Sarah Doody adds that recruiters want process detail but not "a white paper" ([Career Strategy Lab](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/how-long-should-ux-case-study-be/)).

Whether a portfolio matters at all is a fair question. In Gupta's June 2024 survey of 325 PMs, **16% had a portfolio**, roughly 10% in the Americas against **nearly 40% in Asia and the Middle East**, and he says "in India, many PM job postings even ask for one" ([Aakash Gupta, The Rise of the PM Portfolio](https://aakashgupta.medium.com/the-rise-of-the-pm-portfolio-f29c75c3d01c)). The survey is small, self-reported and run by someone who sells PM courses. The sceptical view, from a hiring manager on Blind, is that most experienced PMs have none and that new-grad portfolios copied from tutorials "don't mean much" ([Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/do-you-look-at-candidates-portfolio-zlbuydfd)). Both points can hold: the resume remains the gate, and the portfolio pays off for candidates whose resume lacks a PM title, provided the work is specific and original. For APMs, one guide says "2-3 teardowns or side projects is more valuable than a perfect GPA with nothing to show," and that hiring managers "don't expect pixel-perfect mockups or shipped MVPs" ([Product Alliance](https://www.productalliance.com/post/associate-product-manager-apm-programs-in-2026-the-complete-guide)).

## Site structure is simple: four pages, three or four projects, contact everywhere

Because no real site could be fetched, nav order and page counts for named portfolios are unverified. What the guides agree on is a small standard set: **Case studies (Work), About, Resume (often a PDF) and Contact or LinkedIn**, with a Writing section more common on senior portfolios ([Product School](https://productschool.com/blog/career-development/product-management-portfolio-examples); [HelloPM](https://hellopm.co/product-manager-portfolio-guide/)). HelloPM notes that recruiters "rarely read more than 3-4 case studies in detail," and guides range from 3 to 5 projects for general PM roles to 2 to 3 for APMs ([HelloPM](https://hellopm.co/product-manager-portfolio-guide/); [Product Alliance](https://www.productalliance.com/post/associate-product-manager-apm-programs-in-2026-the-complete-guide)). An early-career, MBA-style layout seen in a GitHub repo description uses hero, about, experience timeline, key projects, skills, case competitions, education and contact ([GitHub: Ashutosh-nagaria/AN-website](https://github.com/Ashutosh-nagaria/AN-website)), though that is a low-profile example, not a proven one.

The homepage should lead with the strongest selling point: experience if the employers are impressive, projects if the candidate is early-career with strong work ([Resumemate](https://www.resumemate.io/blog/create-a-portfolio-website-for-resume-links-and-why/)). One commonly cited example puts main skills at the top of the intro next to a LinkedIn link ([CareerFoundry](https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/product-management/product-manager-portfolio/)). The same guide warns that time on flashy design is poorly spent; clarity wins. My inference, not observed on a specific site, is that the best opening is one positioning line (what kind of PM, which domain) with two or three proof points directly under it (company names, one headline metric each), then the case study cards, each carrying an outcome title and a project-type tag.

On resume and contact, the advice is unanimous and easy to follow. Link the resume and LinkedIn from the site, make contact findable "without hunting for an email address" ([Resumemate](https://www.resumemate.io/blog/create-a-portfolio-website-for-resume-links-and-why/)), and put the portfolio URL in the resume header and LinkedIn's Website field ([Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/portfolio-on-resume/)). Keep the resume plain and let the portfolio carry depth. The resume PDF must match the site's numbers exactly, because a mismatch is the kind of thing an interviewer notices. The About page gets less attention in the sources than any other section; guides describe it simply as a background summary and key skills ([Product School](https://productschool.com/blog/career-development/product-management-portfolio-examples)). I found no evidence on what makes an About page effective, so the working assumption is that it should explain the path into product in a few sentences, state what kind of problems the candidate wants to work on, and avoid restating the resume.

## Case studies win on the decision trail, not the activity list

Almost every framework runs context, problem, approach, outcome, reflection. They differ on whether the result comes first and whether trade-offs get a section. Laura Klein's point frames the PM version: designers have portfolios and engineers have GitHub, but "as a PM, what you make most often is decisions" ([Users Know](https://www.usersknow.com/blog/2019/4/9/portfolios-for-product-managers)). The structure that best fits this is CraftUp's **decision trail: problem, evidence, alternatives, decision, execution, metrics, learning**, with the instruction to "include the smallest set of artifacts that proves the decisions" ([CraftUp](https://craftuplearn.com/blog/product-manager-portfolio-projects-get-hired)). IGotAnOffer argues STAR is "not the best" for PM work and proposes SPSIL (Situation, Problem, Solution, Impact, Lessons) ([IGotAnOffer](https://igotanoffer.com/en/advice/star-method-product-manager-interview)). The Minto pyramid adds the result-first header that skimmers need ([UX Playbook](https://uxplaybook.org/articles/ux-case-study-minto-pyramid-structure-guide)). No source compares these empirically.

| Framework | Order | Use for PMs | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| STAR | Situation, Task, Action, Result | Matches behavioural interviews | "Action" hides the decision; no trade-offs or lessons |
| SPSIL | Situation, Problem, Solution, Impact, Lessons | Adds reflection | No explicit options considered |
| Decision trail | Problem, evidence, alternatives, decision, execution, metrics, learning | Best match for "PMs make decisions" | Longer, needs editing discipline |
| Minto (result first) | Result, key arguments, detail | Best for skimmers | Weak when there is no clear result |

Combining them gives a template: **TL;DR snapshot, context, problem with evidence, insight, options and trade-offs, decision, execution (brief), results against a baseline, learnings and next steps.** The options and trade-offs section is what most separates a PM case study from a design one, and it is exactly what interviews probe. Guides for APMs say case studies should cover "what problem they identified, what tradeoffs they considered, and what success would look like" ([Product Alliance](https://www.productalliance.com/post/associate-product-manager-apm-programs-in-2026-the-complete-guide)), and Gupta says offer-winning portfolios explain "the why behind decisions" and are tailored to the role and company ([Aakash Gupta](https://www.aakashg.com/product-manager-portfolio-examples/)). Leslie Yang, a Lyft design manager, asks for "clear writing about user problems, business goals, research performed, and decisions made along the way" ([Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)). Artifacts should each prove one decision: a funnel chart shows where to focus, a prioritisation table shows what was cut, an experiment card shows how you knew. Personas and journey maps belong only where they changed a decision.

Role clarity is a near-universal requirement. Strong portfolios name the candidate's function and the other roles on the team; when the role is missing, hiring managers ask in interviews and may suspect the omission was deliberate ([The Crit](https://www.thecrit.co/resources/portfolio-project-examples)). Crediting the team makes your own contribution clearer, not smaller ([Learn UI Design](https://www.learnui.design/blog/great-ux-ui-portfolio-case-study.html)), and any case study that fails the "what specifically did you do" test should be cut ([Creative Project Management](https://www.creativeprojectmanagement.org/blog/how-to-build-a-creative-project-management-portfolio-that-stands-out)). Unfinished or partly failed work is acceptable when framed as a learning loop; "a project that shows a clean learning loop can be more credible than one that makes you look perfect" ([CraftUp](https://craftuplearn.com/blog/product-manager-portfolio-projects-get-hired)), and a write-up of a project that did not work should end with "the sharper question your work actually answered" ([Apurva Bhure](https://apurvabhure.substack.com/p/how-do-you-write-a-case-study-about)).

## Credible metrics need a baseline, a window and an honest label

Metrics are the most cited signal. Guides call for "metric-driven" portfolios ([Leland](https://www.joinleland.com/library/a/product-manager-portfolio)), and Shreyas Doshi's widely repeated position is that PMs should be judged on **outcomes (metrics moved, problems solved), not outputs (features shipped, docs written)** ([Lenny's Podcast summary](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/episode-3-shreyas-doshi)). "Claiming success but showing no proof" is named as a major red flag ([Chris Nguyen](https://uxchrisnguyen.medium.com/11-ux-portfolio-red-flags-that-are-killing-your-career-371635410996)). The most specific guidance on credibility comes from an agency source rather than PM hiring: document **baseline, intervention and outcome**, over a stated comparison window, with one primary KPI and two or three supporting signals ([Lazarev.agency](https://www.lazarev.agency/articles/ux-roi-case-studies)). The rest is my inference, because no PM source addresses it: label each figure as actual, estimated or projected; say how it was measured (A/B test, pre/post, cohort); say "the pilot showed" rather than "I drove" when there was no control; and mention a guardrail you watched. A sceptical PM interviewer will ask about seasonality and concurrent changes in any pre/post comparison, so naming them first reads as judgement. For concept or student work, leading indicators (task success in a prototype test, stated intent, pilot sign-ups) should be shown as such and not dressed up as business outcomes.

## Confidential work: anonymise, index the numbers, keep the logic public

All NDA guidance found comes from design sources, but it transfers cleanly. Replace brand names, logos and distinctive UI, because "a stray logo in a corner... can reveal client identity" ([Wonderlist](https://www.wonderlist.design/insights/showcasing-work-under-nda)). "Use percentages if you must talk about numerical metrics" ([IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/how-to-handle-non-disclosure-agreements-ndas-when-you-write-your-ux-case-study)), and focus on challenge, approach and outcomes without proprietary specifics ([IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/keep-it-confidential-how-to-showcase-your-nda-protected-design-work)). Being open about NDA status is said to signal trustworthiness ([IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/keep-it-confidential-how-to-showcase-your-nda-protected-design-work)), and asking permission, sometimes after launch or after time passes, is an option ([freelancermap](https://www.freelancermap.com/blog/can-i-share-nda-protected-work-on-my-portfolio-tips-and-advice/)). Password protection is acceptable for genuinely sensitive work but adds friction that recruiters resent, especially when the password is not on the resume ([Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/password-protected-portfolios-are-driving-me-crazy-jlg5y1gp)). Leslie Yang's fix is to put the link and password on both the application and the resume ([Leslie Yang](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)); a hybrid of public work plus a private section is another ([Designfolio](https://designfolio.substack.com/p/portfolio-tips-ux)). For PMs the decision logic is what is being evaluated and is rarely the confidential part, so keep it public and redact only the numbers and identifiers that are sensitive. Careless disclosure is a double red flag for a PM, since data judgement is itself tested.

## Red flags and credibility signals, ranked by how often they are cited

The most cited red flags are **claims without numbers, buzzwords and tool lists without project context, unclear personal role, and generic or tutorial-copied projects** ([HiringFast](https://www.hiringfa.st/blog/resume-red-flags/how-to-spot-weak-project-manager-resumes); [Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/do-you-look-at-candidates-portfolio-zlbuydfd)). Skill bars are called "the visual equivalent of a stock photo" ([Hiration](https://www.hiration.com/blog/skill-bars-resume/)). Cluttered, hard-to-scan layouts did badly in the resume eye-tracking data ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/)). "Coming soon" pages, overlong case studies and design-only portfolios are not named as PM complaints in any source found; they follow by inference from the skim data and from the point that hiring managers want thought process, not mockups.

On the positive side, verifiable evidence beats claims. Product School calls stakeholder testimonials "a huge asset" and says screenshots and real-world photos "turn abstract product work into tangible evidence" ([Product School](https://productschool.com/blog/career-development/product-management-portfolio-examples)). Gupta says AI PM leaders will check a linked GitHub and pushes working prototypes ([Aakash Gupta, Medium](https://aakashgupta.medium.com/only-17-of-pms-have-a-portfolio-heres-how-to-build-one-that-actually-gets-you-hired-3fed3e2f438a)). Side projects are legitimate evidence when employment work cannot be shared ([ProductPeople](https://productpeople.co/guides/building-portfolio)), and proactive teardowns of a target company's product have reportedly led to interviews ([Aakash Gupta](https://www.aakashg.com/product-manager-portfolio-examples/)). None of these sources weighs one signal against another. My rough ordering, which is inference: a live product or prototype link with real usage, then a verifiable public metric, then a named testimonial, then screenshots of real work, then a self-authored teardown or PRD, then unsupported claims.

## Practical checklist

This checklist compresses the principles above. Items marked (inferred) come from my synthesis rather than a cited source.

| Area | Do this |
|---|---|
| Pages | Home, Work (case studies), About, Resume PDF, Contact. Nothing else unless it carries evidence. |
| Homepage | One positioning line; 2 to 3 proof points under it (inferred); 3 to 4 case study cards, best first. |
| Card | Outcome as title; project-type tag (Internship, Live project, Side project); role; one headline metric. |
| Case study top | Snapshot box: role, team, timeline, status (shipped, pilot, concept), problem in one line, 2 to 3 decisions, headline metrics with baseline. |
| Case study body | Context, problem with evidence, insight, options and trade-offs, decision, brief execution, results, learnings, next steps. Headings that state conclusions (inferred). |
| Length | Flagship about 1,000 to 1,500 words; supporting projects 400 to 800 (inferred from the 800 to 1,500 range). |
| Artifacts | One annotated visual per key decision. Crop PRDs to their summary. Link full files as optional. |
| Metrics | Baseline, window, method, label (actual, estimated, projected), attribution wording, one guardrail (inferred). |
| Role | "I" for your decisions, "we" for team results; name other roles. |
| NDA | Anonymise or get sign-off, index or percentage the numbers, say it is under NDA, keep the reasoning public; if password-gated, put the password on the resume. |
| Resume and contact | Email, LinkedIn and Resume PDF in the nav or header on every page and in the footer; portfolio URL in resume header and LinkedIn. Resume numbers match the site. |
| About | Short path into product, the problems you want to work on; no resume restatement (inferred). |
| Skills and tools | Show each tool inside a project where it was used; no skill bars or bare tool lists. |
| Credibility | Live links, working prototypes, real usage numbers, attributed testimonials. |
| Remove | Placeholder or "coming soon" pages, buzzwords, unverifiable claims, anything that fails "what did you specifically do". |
| Test | The 30-second recall test on each case study with someone outside product. |

## Applying this to Radhika Goel

Radhika's context shapes how the portfolio will be used. She is in ISB's PGP Young Leaders track, which is closer to an early-career or APM profile than the regular PGP cohort the sources describe as moving into mid-level PM roles ([GoalisB](https://www.goalisb.com/post/isb-mba-career-options-product-manager)). Campus shortlists are resume-driven, so the portfolio will mostly be read by interviewers after shortlisting, which argues for strong depth under a skimmable top layer and a short URL printed in the CV header. She has what the APM guidance asks for: two real-company projects and one side project with real users. The recommended order is Tata Consumer Products first, Country Delight second, Elective Planner third, with JP Morgan Chase as an experience line on the homepage and resume until its details exist.

**Positioning line (draft):** "Product manager in training at ISB (IT & Ops). I build data and AI tools for field teams and test them with the people who have to use them." Proof points under it: the TCP pilot headline, the CD prototype, the Elective Planner adoption number. This is a draft to adjust; the point is one line that names the kind of PM work plus immediate evidence.

### Tata Consumer Products: the flagship, and the strongest decision story

This project has everything the sources ask for: a funnel, a baseline comparison, a field insight that contradicted the obvious reading, and a product decision that followed from it. The case study should lead with the outcome and the insight together. A suggested title: "An AI outlet pipeline that sales reps stopped trusting, and how we fixed it." The snapshot would state: summer internship, her role, team, timeline, status (pilot), headline results of **~600 outlets billed in 2 months and ~50% lift in monthly outlet additions vs FY25**, and the key finding that **33% of recommended stores were never visited** because reps stopped trusting the list.

The funnel is the core artifact: 3,305 suggested, 2,217 visited, 569 billed. That is a 67% visit rate, a 26% visit-to-bill rate and 17% end to end, and the 1,088 unvisited stores are the gap the insight explains. Two things need resolving before publishing. First, the headline says ~600 billed and the funnel says 569; the site must explain the difference (a different time window or scope) or use one number consistently, since the resume will be compared against it. Second, the project is confidential, so she should confirm with TCP what is cleared. If absolute counts are not cleared, index the funnel (suggested = 100, visited = 67, billed = 17) and keep the percentages, which carry the whole argument. Describe the company by name only if permitted; otherwise "a large Indian FMCG company."

The decision section is where this case study wins. The pipeline (Google Places sourcing, deduplication, distributor mapping) is execution; the decision is what she did after 50+ rep interviews showed the problem was trust, not data quality alone. The options-and-trade-offs block should set out what she considered (for example, improving the model's precision, adding rep incentives, or the human-in-the-loop feedback flow she proposed) and why the feedback loop won. The metrics section should say plainly that the ~50% lift is a pre/post comparison against FY25 without a control group, name seasonality and any concurrent sales pushes as confounders, and label the feedback flow as proposed, not shipped. "What I would do next" should list the metrics that would show the feedback flow working, such as visit rate on recommended stores and rep-flagged false positives. Her own role versus the TCP team's needs one clear line.

### Country Delight: show the constraint that shaped the design

The sources say concept work is credible when it has real users and explicit constraints, and this project has both. The story is the GST constraint changing the loyalty design, which is a clean trade-off: lead with it. A suggested title: "Designing a loyalty system around a tax constraint to move Country Delight customers beyond milk." The snapshot should tag it as a one-month live project, state the team and her role, and label the status as a recommendation with clickable prototypes, not a launched product.

The body should follow the decision trail: the category-penetration problem, the 14 customer interviews and what they showed, personas only if they changed the decision, the ~15 ideas and the impact/feasibility matrix as the prioritisation artifact, then the tiered CD Coins system and specifically how the GST constraint ruled out or reshaped simpler options. Link the live prototypes directly; a working prototype sits high on the credibility ladder. Because there is no business outcome, name the leading indicators instead: what the client said about the recommendation, any usability test results on the prototype, and the success metrics she would track after launch (for example, share of milk-only customers adding a second category). Checking with Country Delight on what can be published is also needed, since live-project briefs often carry their own confidentiality terms.

### Elective Planner: the verifiable proof of building and shipping

This is the most verifiable item she has, and it answers the "tutorial copy" red flag because real students chose to use it. It needs numbers to work: how many students used it, what share of the batch, over what period, repeat usage, and any feedback that led to a change. Without those, "adopted by students across the college" reads as a claim. The case study can be short (400 to 800 words): the problem students had choosing electives, what she built with AI tools and which build decisions were hers, how it spread, and what she changed after launch. A live link and a screenshot are essential. The AI-built angle is timely given current interest in AI prototyping among PM hiring managers, but the write-up should be about the product decisions, not the tooling.

### JP Morgan Chase, tools and the rest of the site

Until the JP Morgan Chase AWM Risk details are available, list it as an experience line with dates and one sentence of scope, and do not create a placeholder case study page. When details arrive, decide whether it has a product-shaped decision worth a case study; risk work often carries tighter confidentiality, so the NDA rules apply with more force. Figma, SQL and Illustrator should appear inside the projects where they were used (SQL in the TCP funnel analysis if that is where she used it, Figma in the CD prototypes) and on the About or resume page as a plain line, never as skill bars. The About page should explain in a few sentences why an IT & Ops student is moving toward product and what kinds of problems she wants to work on. Email, LinkedIn and the resume PDF go in the header of every page, and the resume numbers must match the site exactly. If she can get a short attributed quote from a TCP manager or the Country Delight project lead, it would add a credibility signal no other part of the site provides.

## Conclusion

The evidence base for PM portfolios is thin: search summaries of opinion pieces, one small survey, and a resume eye-tracking study borrowed by analogy. What survives that weakness is a consistent set of requirements that no source contradicts, and they point less toward design polish than toward editorial discipline: outcome-first titles, a snapshot a reviewer can absorb in 30 seconds, and a decision trail with the rejected options visible. For a candidate at Radhika's stage, the gap between a forgettable portfolio and a strong one is rarely the amount of work. It is whether the work is framed as decisions with evidence, labelled honestly as pilot, concept or side project, and backed by numbers that reconcile across the site and the resume.

Her TCP project carries an unusually strong interview hook because its most important finding is a failure of the first version: a third of the recommendations went unvisited. Leading with that, rather than hiding it behind the 50% lift, matches the sources' view that a clean learning loop reads as more credible than a flawless story, and it hands interviewers the question she is best prepared to answer. The immediate open items are the ~600 versus 569 discrepancy, clearance from TCP and Country Delight, adoption numbers for the Elective Planner, and the JP Morgan Chase details. Separately, a rerun of the site-teardown research from an environment where portfolio sites can be fetched would replace the inferred nav and homepage patterns with observed ones.
